Your heart is pounding. Your chest is tight. Your brain is screaming, *do something. Fix it. Text them. Leave. Say the thing. Right now. RIGHT NOW.*

Everything in your body is telling you this is an emergency.

It's not. It's a feeling. And I know that sounds f\*cking impossible when you're in it, so let's talk about why.

## First, I'm not qualified

You know the drill. I'm not a professional, I'm not licensed, and I can't diagnose or treat anything. I'm someone who has spent years learning her own patterns, and this is one of the biggest ones I've found. Take what's useful, and bring the rest to a qualified professional.

## Why a feeling feels like a fire alarm

When a big emotion hits, your nervous system doesn't send a polite memo. It sounds an alarm. Your body gets ready to act: faster heartbeat, shallow breathing, tight muscles, racing thoughts. That's the same machinery that's supposed to get you out of real danger.

The problem is that the alarm doesn't know the difference between a bear and an unanswered text. It just knows something feels intense, so it screams *act now.*

For a lot of us with BPD, the alarm is sensitive and slow to settle. So the urge to do something, anything, to make the feeling stop can feel completely unbearable.

And that's where the damage usually happens. Not in the feeling itself, but in what we do to escape it.

## The urge is not the instruction

Here's the part I wish somebody had told me twenty years ago.

An urge is not an order. You can feel the urge to send the text, burn the bridge, quit the job, or disappear, and you do not have to obey it.

The feeling is real. The urge is real. But the feeling is information, and the urge is just your nervous system asking for relief the fastest way it knows how.

Your emotions aren't emergencies. They're data.

Data tells you what's going on inside. It doesn't tell you what to do about it. That part is your call.

## What sitting with it actually looks like

Sitting with an emotion does not mean wallowing, and it does not mean agreeing with everything the feeling says. It means staying present with the experience instead of racing to get rid of it.

Here's how I practice.

**Name it.** "Right now I'm feeling rejected." "This is panic." "This is shame." Putting words on a feeling gives your brain something to hold besides the flood.

**Find it in your body.** Where is it? Chest? Throat? Stomach? Get curious about it like a scientist. Tight, hot, heavy, buzzing. You're observing, not fixing.

**Breathe slower than feels natural.** Longer out than in. You're not trying to force calm. You're telling your body the bear is not actually in the room.

**Remind yourself it's a wave.** Feelings rise, peak, and fall. They tend to crest and move through when we stop feeding them with panic and story. You've survived every one of them so far. That's a perfect record.

**Say the dialectic.** "I feel terrified, and I am safe right now." Both are true. Hold both.

**Wait before you act.** If the urge is to send, say, or do something permanent, give it time. A feeling that is loud at 11 p.m. often looks very different the next morning. You can always do the thing later. You can't unsend it.

## This is not about being calm all the time

I'm not asking you to become some serene, tea sipping monk who never gets rattled. Who the f\*ck has time for that?

You're going to feel things at full volume. That's part of who you are, and I'm not asking you to shrink it. I'm asking you to learn to feel it without letting it drive the car.

That's the difference between being overwhelmed by a feeling and being in relationship with it.

## When you can't do it alone

Some feelings are too big to sit with solo, and that's okay. Reach for support when you need it. That might be a trusted person, a therapist who understands BPD, or a crisis line if you're in real danger. If you ever feel unsafe, that is a real emergency and you deserve real help, right away. In the U.S. you can call or text 988 anytime.

Asking for help is not failing at this. It's part of the skill.

## Your turn

Next time the alarm goes off, try one thing. Just one. Name it. Say, "This is a feeling, not an emergency." Then give it five minutes before you do anything.

Not perfect. Just better.

You are allowed to feel it all. And you are strong enough to let it pass through.

That's the work. And we're going to keep figuring it out together.
