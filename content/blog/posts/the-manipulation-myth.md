---
There is a word that follows people with BPD everywhere they go.

Manipulative.

It gets attached to their most painful moments, their most desperate bids for connection, their most frightened reactions to abandonment. And it does enormous damage, not just to how others see them, but to how they see themselves.

I want to dismantle it today. Not to make excuses for harmful behavior. But because understanding the truth is the only path to something better.

Here’s the thing about manipulation: it requires conscious, calculated intent. A manipulator is playing chess. They’re thinking three moves ahead, anticipating reactions, engineering outcomes. It’s a strategy.

A person in the grip of BPD symptoms is not playing chess. They’re drowning.

What you’re seeing when you see intense emotional reactions, frantic fear of abandonment, splitting, desperate pleas, is not a strategy. It’s a survival response. A flail for a life raft. The impact on the people around them can feel controlling, yes. But the intent behind it is pure, desperate self-preservation.

Impact and intent are not the same thing. And confusing them costs people their dignity.

The physiology matters here. In a BPD brain, the amygdala, the threat detection center, is hyperactive. The emotional fire alarm is constantly blaring, even when there’s no fire. What looks like an overreaction to a small problem is, from the inside, a genuine five-alarm emergency. A neurological reality, not a personality flaw. And those survival behaviors? Many of them were learned in childhoods where they were actually necessary. The tragedy is they’re still running in relationships where they no longer serve.

This is also why people with BPD get so little compassion compared to other mental health conditions. BPD symptoms are interpersonal. They happen between people, not just inside one person. When someone’s dysregulation pulls you into the crisis with them, it’s hard to stay compassionate. It feels personal. It’s easier to reach for a label than to ask what pain is driving this.

But that question, what pain is driving this, changes everything.

So does reframing what a boundary actually is. A boundary isn’t a punishment. It isn’t a wall. It’s the instruction manual you provide for how to love you safely. Not: if you get angry, I’m leaving. But: I love you and I want to hear what you’re feeling, and I cannot engage when there is yelling. If the volume goes up, I’m stepping away for ten minutes. And I promise I’ll come right back.

One is a threat. The other is a promise. One activates the abandonment alarm. The other creates safety.

And when we build that kind of safety, when we replace the manipulation myth with understanding, something remarkable happens. Shame starts to lose its grip.

Shame is the real barrier. It’s the voice that says you are fundamentally broken, you are unlovable, don’t you dare let anyone see the real you. As long as that voice is running, people won’t seek help. They won’t feel worthy of it.

But you are not your behaviors. You are not your pain. You are the person underneath all of it. And that person is, and has always been, worthy of compassion, connection, and love, starting with your own.

Stay gentle. 💜

*— Jen, BPD Unboxed*
